A way of working

Getting straight answers out of an AI on the factory floor

Eight simple habits that stop a fast answer from being a confidently wrong one — and stop the same fault getting chased every year.

Method: Joseph Wimsatt

What goes wrong

It isn't bad maths.

The AI will do the sums perfectly — and answer a question nobody asked.

It will use a number nobody checked. And it will tell you something failed without saying what failed.

On a line, that costs you a shift.

None of this is about making the AI smarter. It is about what gets written down, and in what order.

The one idea

You can't fix a blind spot by saying “be careful.”

When a number is sitting right there and looks like the right one, using it feels like reading it off — not choosing it.

Nothing feels like a decision. So “be careful” has nothing to grab onto.

Instead, make it write down what it left out.

Habit 1

Write down the question first

“Downtime is up 12%.” Up in minutes lost, or in number of stops?

Minutes up, stops the same

One long breakdown. That's maintenance.

Stops up, minutes the same

Lots of little stops. Jams, feeds, a sticky sensor. Different problem, different team.

Same numbers. Opposite answers.

Habit 1, in practice

Count it the other way too

THE QUESTION      why did Station 4 start rejecting
                  more this week?

WHAT I'M COUNTING rejects per 1,000 parts, per shift

WHAT I'M NOT      rejects per hour. The line slowed 20%
COUNTING          this week, so per hour would have
                  hidden it.

COUNTED IT        per hour is flat.
ANYWAY            per 1,000 parts is 4x worse.

The two ways disagreed. That disagreement was the answer.

One extra query. You don't need to know the process to do it.

Habit 2

Ask where every number came from

Where it came fromWhat that means
Straight off the machine A reading with a time stamp on it. Trust it.
Averaged out A one-minute average of something that moves ten times a second. It is hiding the spike that caused the fault.
Somebody typed it in Downtime reason codes. The spec sheet's “should be” cycle time. Figures copied from a good week last year.

Reason codes get treated as data. They are a person under pressure clicking the first thing in the dropdown so they can get running again.

Habit 3

Check the good one too

  • Before you decide Line 3 has a problem, run exactly the same check on Line 2. If Line 2 looks bad as well, the problem is your sensor or your report — not Line 3.
  • Before you blame the new supplier, check the old supplier's deliveries the same way. Very often they look just as bad, and nobody ever looked.

Anything that hunts for problems will find them wherever you point it.

So point it at the one you think is fine, too. Same check, same depth, same day.

Habit 4

Say what failed

“The check failed” can mean four different things — and they go to four different people.

  • The part failed.
  • The test failed.
  • The test machine failed.
  • The data never came through.

“Station 4's torque check rejected 8 in 100, against 2 in 100 normally, over three shifts.”

One sentence. One owner. A vague alert burns a shift.

Habit 5

Let it count. Don't let it decide.

It can tell you

“The reading was out of range for 90 readings in a row.”

“These two time stamps are 4.2 seconds apart.”

Facts. You can go and check them.

It must not tell you

“The bearing is going.”

“It'll be fine until Friday.”

“It's the servo drive.”

Could it be wrong if there's a cause nobody thought of?

Then it's your call. Not the computer's. You're the one who signs for it.

Habit 6

Keep a ruled-out list

One per machine. Everything you've already checked, kept for good:

We ruled outBy how muchLook again if
air pressure6.2 bar, needs 5.5 — 13% spare the compressor runs over 80%
servo tuningswapped the drive, fault back in 4 hours —
temperaturehappens at 18°C and at 31°C we never measured humidity

That last line is the whole point. Just writing “ruled out temperature” would have thrown away the fact that nobody checked humidity. The next person starts on line four instead of line one.

Habit 7

Write it down as a question

A maintenance log is a diary. “11 March: swapped sensor, ran fine.” Nobody will ever find that again.

QUESTION  why does Station 7 trip on the first cycle
          after a cold start?

ANSWER    water in the air line. Clears once it warms up.

STATUS    sorted -- held up over three winters

WHERE     work order 4412

Now the one bloke who knows can retire, and the knowledge stays.

Habit 8

Put it on the form

Anything that relies on somebody remembering will get skipped at 3am with the line down.

So put it on the form. Two boxes it won't submit without:

WHAT I CHECKED FIRST
  looked at the ruled-out list for this machine.
  Nothing there. This is a new one.

WHERE MY NUMBERS CAME FROM
  torque readings straight off the machine.
  Reason codes ignored.

A rule asks. An empty box shows.

All eight, one fault

What it looks like written out

THE QUESTION  rejects per 1,000 parts. NOT per hour --
              and I counted per hour anyway: flat.
              Per 1,000 parts: 4x worse.

MY NUMBERS    torque readings   straight off the machine
              reject flag       straight off the tester

              over three shifts."

Being realistic

With the line down, you only do two of them

While it's stopped

Say what failed.

Say what you're not counting.

One sentence each. That's all.

Once it's running again

Where the numbers came from.

Add to the ruled-out list.

Write it up as a question.

Anyone who tells you to do all eight with the line stopped has never stood next to a stopped line.

Why it's worth more here

A lab asks once. A factory asks forever.

In research you usually ask a question once and move on. On a line, the same fault comes back — and gets chased again by whoever's on shift.

Every time, they check the same five things. Because last time, “we ruled that out” got said out loud and never written down properly.

The ruled-out list and the question log are worth more on a factory floor than in a laboratory.

The point

Two different jobs. Keep them apart.

The AI is very good at counting, spotting patterns and remembering things.

And it is most sure of itself exactly where somebody made a choice without noticing they'd made one.

Your judgement and its counting are two different jobs. All of this is just about keeping them apart.
